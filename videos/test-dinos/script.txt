No photograph of a living Tyrannosaurus rex has ever existed. No witness. No preserved muscle. No skin sample. No color chart. Almost all of the soft tissue is gone. What's left is mostly rock.

And yet, walk into almost any natural history museum on Earth, and you'll find a Tyrannosaurus standing there. Fully fleshed, textured, colored, mid-stride, looking back at you like someone simply forgot to take the photograph.

Here's the uncomfortable part. We've gotten this badly wrong before.

In the 19th century, scientists working on one of the first dinosaurs ever reconstructed, Iguanodon, found a strange, spike-shaped bone among the fossils and couldn't quite place it. Their best guess was that it belonged on the animal's face, a horn, sitting on the nose.

For years, that's exactly how Iguanodon was drawn, sculpted, and displayed to the public.

It wasn't stupidity. It was the best possible reading of incomplete evidence.
