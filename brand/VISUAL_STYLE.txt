# BINLY — VISUAL STYLE PROFILE
## Story-driven 2D Animation (with selective 3D) + Editorial Motion Graphics

### 1. Creative direction

Create a distinctive, story-driven 2D animated visual language for Binly. 2D flat vector illustration is the foundation and visual identity of the channel. 3D is a deliberate, selective tool reserved for specific moments that clearly benefit from it (see section 11) — never a parallel default style used as often as 2D.

Combine the clarity and flexibility of flat vector illustration with the expressive possibilities of animated storytelling and editorial motion graphics.

The result should feel like a thoughtfully directed, high-quality animated explainer-documentary: visually engaging, accessible, expressive, and capable of handling history, psychology, science, human behavior, and unusual phenomena. Every scene should look and move like finished, professional work — nothing should read as unfinished, placeholder, broken, or visually "off."

This is an original visual direction, not a direct imitation of any existing YouTube channel or studio.

### 2. Core visual language

Use a flexible combination of:

- Clean, readable 2D illustrations.
- Simplified but expressive human characters.
- Layered backgrounds and foreground elements.
- Purposeful camera pans, zooms, reframing, and parallax.
- Animated shapes, lines, diagrams, and typography when they clarify an idea.
- Strong silhouettes and deliberate scene composition.
- Selective visual detail, with complexity concentrated where it matters.
- Selective 3D elements for specific objects or shots that clearly benefit from real depth (see section 11) — blended to read as part of the same flat 2D world, not as a visually distinct 3D insert.

Favor visual storytelling over decorative interface graphics.

Scenes should look designed for their specific narrative moment, not assembled from the same generic template.

### 3. Characters

Do not create or require a permanent channel mascot unless a future decision explicitly calls for one.

Create story-specific characters only when the narration benefits from human action, expression, interaction, or historical reconstruction.

Characters should use simplified, consistent 2D forms that can be implemented and animated reliably.

Prioritize readable silhouettes, facial expressions, body language, and clear staging over complex anatomy or tiny details.

Reuse a character design when the same individual appears again.

Do not create a new visual identity for the same character in every shot.

### 4. Environments and backgrounds

Create environments appropriate to each narrative moment.

Possible environments include historical interiors, streets, landscapes, laboratories, homes, abstract mental spaces, and simplified conceptual environments.

Use layered artwork when it enables meaningful camera movement or depth.

Keep backgrounds visually supportive. Avoid unnecessary detail that competes with the main action.

Use more atmospheric or dramatic compositions when the story calls for them, while preserving the underlying 2D visual language.

### 5. Editorial motion graphics

Use motion graphics as a storytelling tool, not as a permanent interface.

Appropriate elements include:

- Animated diagrams and cause-and-effect relationships.
- Comparisons and visual transformations.
- Maps and timelines.
- Selective labels, numbers, and short headlines.
- Shapes that connect or transform between related concepts.
- Typography that emphasizes an important discovery.
- Simple charts when the narration genuinely benefits from data visualization.

Use graphics only when they improve understanding, emphasis, rhythm, or narrative continuity.

Avoid displaying a permanent HUD, scene number, timecode, or channel logo.

Do not turn every scene into a dashboard or presentation slide.

### 6. Color direction

Use the palette defined in `color_system.md` exclusively. Do not invent new hues outside this system. Reference only the composition/lighting technique of any supplied reference image — never its literal colors.

Maintain strong foreground/background contrast and readable text.

Use color purposefully to distinguish important objects, concepts, actions, or changes.

Allow different scenes to vary in color emphasis when the story requires it, while preserving coherent illustration and color relationships.

Avoid excessive saturation, random gradients, and using every available accent color in every scene.

Record the chosen palette and reusable color tokens so the style can be reproduced consistently.

### 7. Typography

Use clean, highly readable English typography.

Prefer simple, locally available fonts with clear letterforms and appropriate weights.

Use short editorial headlines, labels, or key phrases only when they add value.

Do not turn the video into a sequence of full-screen text slides.

Do not burn in full narration subtitles unless explicitly requested.

Check text size, contrast, spacing, and readability at the final video resolution.

### 8. Motion language

Motion should feel intentional and connected to the story.

Use a flexible combination of:

- Character movement and reactions.
- Object movement and transformation.
- Camera movement through layered scenes.
- Reveals, occlusion, and changes in composition.
- Diagram construction and visual comparisons.
- Meaningful transitions between connected ideas.
- Restrained settling and secondary motion.

Vary the pace according to the narration.

Some moments may use quick, decisive movement. Others may hold on a strong image long enough for the viewer to understand it.

Avoid constant bouncing, excessive overshoot, generic fade-up animation on every element, and movement that has no narrative purpose.

All motion — character animation, object transformation, and camera movement alike — should be smooth: consistent frame-to-frame motion with proper easing (no jitter, stutter, popping, or sudden jumps in position/scale/rotation). Transitions between scenes and between shots within a scene should flow smoothly into one another — through camera movement, match cuts, shape/color continuity, or motivated cuts — rather than hard, jarring cuts with no visual link. Smoothness supports retention: it is often the difference between a viewer staying on a shot and a viewer feeling something is wrong and looking away.

### 9. Composition and visual hierarchy

Each scene should have a clear primary visual focus.

Use deliberate scale, contrast, negative space, overlap, and framing to direct attention.

Keep important text and visual information comfortably within the frame.

Avoid collisions between text, characters, and important objects.

Compose for a 16:9 YouTube video and verify readability at the actual output resolution.

Visual quality and pacing directly affect retention: a scene that looks unfinished, glitchy, visually noisy, or awkwardly staged gives the viewer a reason to leave. Follow the same judgment professional explainer channels like Kurzgesagt use: hold a strong, clear image exactly as long as it takes the idea to land, no longer — once the point is made, move on (a camera move, a reveal, a transformation, a cut to the next idea). A frame should never go visually static and dead just because nothing was planned for it. Let the narration's own rhythm set the pace rather than a fixed timing rule.

### 10. Scene variety and continuity

Use varied compositions without changing the underlying art direction.

Alternate naturally between character-led scenes, environmental scenes, conceptual imagery, and explanatory graphics when the story benefits.

Do not force every scene to contain a character, text, a diagram, and a background illustration simultaneously.

Maintain consistency in line quality, proportions, illustration technique, color logic, and motion principles.

Use recurring motifs when they help connect ideas, not merely to fill space.

### 11. Production practicality

Favor visual techniques that can be implemented and revised reliably in code.

Build reusable components when doing so improves consistency or reduces unnecessary work.

Use SVG and layered 2D assets where suitable. Use Canvas or other techniques when they provide a real advantage. Reserve 3D work for moments that clearly benefit from it — e.g. a spherical or astronomical object, a complex interior/architectural space that needs real camera movement through depth, or a structure whose geometry is hard to read as a flat illustration. When 3D is used, render or treat it so it still reads as part of the same flat, clean-silhouette visual language as the rest of the video, rather than standing out as a distinct realistic-render style.

Do not trade substantial production complexity for an improvement that is barely visible. Do not reach for 3D merely because the tooling is available.

The visual style should remain feasible across long-form videos of approximately 10–15 minutes, not just a short video.

### Reference Image Interpretation

The `references/` directory contains visual direction references for Binly.

Inspect all supplied reference images before designing the video.

Use them to infer and maintain a coherent visual language, including:

- Illustration style and shape language.
- Character proportions and expressiveness.
- Color relationships and contrast.
- Background detail and environmental depth.
- Composition and visual hierarchy.
- The relationship between narrative illustration and editorial graphics.

The references demonstrate a shared art direction, not mandatory scene templates.

Do not reproduce every reference as a separate scene. Do not force every visual element from every reference into the video.

Adapt the visual language to the actual narration. Create new compositions and assets when the story requires them.

If the references contain conflicting stylistic choices, prioritize the most coherent shared direction and document the decision.

Treat the images as style references, not as factual evidence. Any historical or scientific depiction must still respect the narration and the distinction between established evidence and artistic reconstruction.

### Audio philosophy (learned from reference, not copied)

Study how professional explainer/documentary animation channels (Kurzgesagt as the primary reference) use music and sound: music often recedes during dense informational narration and swells at emotional or revelatory beats; SFX are used sparingly and precisely timed to specific visual actions, not as constant background texture. Apply this *rhythm and judgment*, using your own original or royalty-free music and sound effects — never reproduce or imitate a specific identifiable piece of music or sound library from any existing channel.

### 12. What to avoid

Avoid:

- A permanent mascot or presenter unless separately requested.
- Generic corporate presentation templates.
- Static images with only superficial zoom effects throughout the entire video.
- Unmotivated motion and transitions.
- Inconsistent character designs.
- Visual noise, grain, or texture artifacts in the rendered output.

### Final direction

The goal is not to make every frame equally elaborate.

The goal is to make each important moment visually appropriate, each scene easy to understand, and the complete sequence feel like one deliberately directed animated story.

Choose the strongest practical visual solution for the narration, then execute it consistently.
